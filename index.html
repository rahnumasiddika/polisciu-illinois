Causal Inference for Observational Studies

This eight-week course introduces students to approaches to causal inference in non-randomized studies. We start with cross-sectional designs and move to designs that observe the same units repeatedly over time. The cross-sectional techniques include matching, regression discontinuity, and natural experiments. The techniques for repeated observations include difference-in-differences, event studies, synthetic controls, and the modern replacements for two-way fixed effects models that researchers use when units adopt a policy at different times. We pair every estimate or test with a sensitivity analysis that asks how far the assumptions behind it would have to fail to change the conclusion. Students apply these methods to real datasets using R. Key texts are Paul Rosenbaum's Design of Observational Studies (2nd ed.) and An Introduction to the Theory of Observational Studies, and Scott Cunningham's Causal Inference: The Mixtape.

Intro to Deep Neural Networks

An applied introduction to deep neural networks (DNNs) for political and social scientists, taught in an accelerated 8-week format. The first half moves from the foundations of supervised machine learning to the architecture of DNNs, treating them as nested compositions of non-linear functions, and teaches hands-on techniques for estimating a DNN's (possibly many) coefficients and optimizing its parameters. The second half covers how DNNs are used to classify and analyze images as data (e.g., convolutional neural networks), text as data (e.g., recurrent neural networks and transformers such as BERT) and, as time permits, other data types.

How to Enroll

Steps depend on your home university and where the course is taught. Choose your university.

Illinois student, course taught at Illinois

  1. Find the course number and section in the course listing above.
  2. Register through Illinois's usual graduate registration system.
  3. If registration is restricted, contact Cara Wong, Director of Graduate Studies (carawong@illinois.edu).

Illinois student, course taught at another member university

  1. Find the course in Self-Service under its Illinois course number (listed with the course on this page).
  2. Register as you would for any Illinois course.
  3. The instructor will share the link for joining remotely and the course materials. For any other questions, you can contact the instructor.

Student from another member university, course taught at Illinois

  1. Ask your department's PoliSci U contact or graduate office which local course number to register under.
  2. Register at your home university.
  3. The instructor will share the link for joining remotely and the course materials. For any other questions, you can contact the instructor.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who can take PoliSci U courses?

Graduate students at the member universities listed at the top of this page. Each course lists its prerequisites; check with the instructor if you're unsure whether you're ready.

Do I pay extra tuition?

Students register at their home university. For questions about tuition or fees, contact your graduate program office.

How are classes delivered?

Classes are taught live by the host instructor. Students at campuses other than the host instructor's join remotely (on Zoom, Google Meet, or another platform chosen by the instructor). Students at the host campus may meet in person or remotely, at the discretion of the host university or department.

Will the course count toward my methods requirements?

Talk with your advisor or the Director of Graduate Studies before you register.

What if the course times clash with my own university's calendar?

The host university's calendar sets the dates. Check the start date and any breaks with the instructor at the start of term.

About PoliSci U

PoliSci U in Advanced Political Methodology is a cooperative graduate training program run by the political science departments at Illinois, Minnesota, Ohio State, and Wisconsin–Madison. Departments share advanced methods courses over interactive video, so students can take specialized courses their own department may not offer that year. The program's history is at the end of this page.

History

In the 1995-96 academic year, an exciting experiment was conducted in distance education. In the fall semester, Illinois, Minnesota, Ohio State, Pennsylvania State, and Wisconsin universities participated in at least one of four interactive multi-site video presentations. In the spring, a class in Time Series Analysis was offered using the same technology with Illinois, Minnesota, Ohio State, and Wisconsin Universities participating.

W. Phillips Shively, Professor of Political Science at the University of Minnesota, was the impetus behind the project. He asked interested Committee on Institutional Cooperation (CIC) political science departments to send representatives to a meeting at the 1995 Midwest Political Science Association annual conference to discuss the possibility of launching it. In the summer of 1995, the group met in Chicago with Roger Clark, Director of the CIC, and Michael Staman, President of CICnet. The project went forward with planning, technical, and monetary assistance from the CIC and the participating universities' departments, administrations, and technical staff. Testing for technical compatibility occurred over the summer.

The project was launched in the fall of 1995 with Chris Achen, Larry Bartels, Henry Brady, and John Freeman, all former presidents of the American Political Science Association's political methodology group, presenting their current research in a series of interactive video conferences.

The project was intended to build a larger community of scholars in quantitative methods, improve the quality of faculty and student work, and overcome a very practical problem: the expensive nature of graduate instruction in the area of quantitative methods (Freeman and Shively 1995). The project also served as a test for the practicality of multi-site interactive video teaching by people who are novices in using the technology. The long-term vision of the project includes a regular seminar series and regular offering of four advanced modeling classes on a rotating basis. John Freeman is a former director of the project, with Janet Box-Steffensmeier currently overseeing the PoliSci U program's efforts. The program coordinator is selected based on the recommendation of each of the participating department chairs.

Political methodology is a growing field. Because of the connections within this field that are fostered by the annual political methodology group summer meeting and the comparative advantage the departments of the CIC enjoy in this area, the project became a promising case study.

The PoliSci U project now extends to regular course offerings. Additionally, PoliSci U continues to offer interactive colloquia from political methodologists at the forefront of advanced quantitative methods.
